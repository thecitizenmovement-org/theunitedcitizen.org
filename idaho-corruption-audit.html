6. The Law as Alleged, Statute by Statute

If the facts are as described, the conduct maps to the following. Each entry states the law and what the filings claim under it, with the screening tier of the underlying claim. Tier A means the filing itself was read firsthand; tier C means the underlying allegation is single-source — the filers’ account. No entry in this section is a finding that the statute was violated. Whether any of them was violated is a question for a prosecutor and, ultimately, for a court — and it turns on proof.

  • Conspiracy against rights, 18 U.S.C. § 241. Two or more persons conspiring to injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate any person in the free exercise of a right secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States. The filings allege coordinated conduct — jail, prosecutor, and defense counsel acting together — to stop the mother’s reporting to federal oversight and to block the detainee’s access to records, counsel, and medical care. Tier: C.
  • Deprivation of rights under color of law, 18 U.S.C. § 242; civil action, 42 U.S.C. § 1983. A person acting under color of law who willfully deprives another of rights secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States. The filings allege willful deprivation throughout: the threat in the cell, the denial of medical care to a disabled pre-trial detainee, the blocking of legal mail and records, the retaliatory transfer and isolation. Willfulness is the line — under § 242 the deprivation must be willful, not negligent. Tier: C.
  • Conspiracy to interfere with civil rights, 42 U.S.C. § 1985. The civil companion to § 241 for the coordinated pattern the filings describe — the threat, the transfer, the mail routing, and the records block, alleged to work together toward the same end. Tier: C.
  • Witness tampering, 18 U.S.C. § 1512. Intimidating, threatening, or corruptly persuading a witness, or destroying, concealing, or falsifying evidence. Up to 20 years. The October 9 threat — “it was not going to end well for either one of us” — was, on the filings’ account, delivered for the express purpose of stopping the mother from contacting the DOJ and other oversight agencies. Tier: C.
  • Retaliation against a witness, victim, or informant, 18 U.S.C. § 1513. Up to 10 years. The filings allege retaliation after the reporting: the transfer four hours away, the separation from the witness population, the punitive isolation, the assault, and the medication denial. The transfer itself is tier B; its purpose is tier C.
  • Obstruction of a pending judicial proceeding, 18 U.S.C. § 1503. Corruptly influencing, intimidating, or impeding the due administration of justice. Up to 10 years. The MacGregor grievance alleges perjury allowed on the record during probable-cause proceedings, false evidence placed on the court record (the nine North Carolina warrants), and a witness examination begun with a false premise repeated three times. No transcript is in hand. Tier: C.
  • Obstruction of proceedings before an agency, 18 U.S.C. § 1505. The filings allege the obstruction began before the DOJ complaints were filed — the mail routing, the concealed HIPAA documentation, the denied power of attorney, the backdated paperwork — aimed at keeping the record from reaching any outside authority. Tier: C.
  • Destroying records to impede a federal investigation, 18 U.S.C. § 1519. Knowingly destroying, altering, concealing, or falsifying a record with intent to impede a federal investigation. Up to 20 years. The filings allege concealment of executed HIPAA release documentation, backdated administrative paperwork, and the withholding of medical records. Tier: C.
  • False statements, 18 U.S.C. § 1001. Knowingly and willfully making a materially false statement in any matter within the jurisdiction of the federal government. Up to 5 years. The grievances allege the assertion of nine active North Carolina felony gun warrants, claimed to be proven entirely false by NCIC records, and staff lying to the family, denying the detainee’s wounds. The NCIC verification is asserted in the grievances, not in hand. Tier: C.
  • Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq. The filings describe a 100% disabled pre-trial detainee and allege disability harassment (officers mocking him as “gimp”), the denial of required healthcare, the refusal of a medical power of attorney, the withholding of prescribed medication and diet, and the absence of ADA policies in the jail. Tier: C.
  • Civil Rights of Institutionalized Persons Act (CRIPA), 42 U.S.C. § 1997. CRIPA gives the Department of Justice authority to investigate a pattern or practice of unconstitutional conditions in state and local institutions, including jails. The DOJ complaints allege exactly a pattern or practice: blocking legal mail, refusing HIPAA requests, failing to provide required healthcare to disabled pre-trial detainees, failure to train, supervise, and discipline, and retaliatory transfers. A CRIPA investigation is not opened by a complaint number alone; it is opened by the Division’s decision. Tier: C for the pattern allegation; the existence of the complaints is A.
  • First Amendment — retaliation for petitioning and reporting. The October 9 threat, on the filings’ account, was explicitly conditioned on stopping the DOJ reporting. The transfer, the isolation, and the mail routing are alleged to be punishment for the same reporting. Tier: C.
  • Fourth Amendment — unreasonable search and seizure of mail. The MacGregor grievance alleges all of the detainee’s incoming and outgoing legal and personal mail was routed through and explicitly approved by the prosecutor’s office before delivery. Legal mail receives heightened protection, and courts treat the interception of an inmate’s legal mail as a serious interference with access to the courts. Tier: C.
  • Fourteenth Amendment — due process and access to the courts. The filings allege the denial of medical care, the blocking of legal mail and records, the withholding of the HIPAA release form, the obstruction of access to counsel, and the retaliatory isolation — each a deprivation of liberty or of access to the courts, without due process, on the filings’ account. Tier: C.
  • Idaho Rules of Professional Conduct — 3.4, 3.8, 4.1, 4.4, 8.4(d). Rule 3.4 (fairness to opposing party and counsel); Rule 3.8 (special responsibilities of a prosecutor); Rule 4.1 (truthfulness in statements to others); Rule 4.4 (respect for the rights of third persons); Rule 8.4(d) (conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice). The MacGregor and Jessup grievances cite these rules for the alleged suppression of exculpatory evidence, the false evidence on the record, the mail interception, and the obstruction of records. A grievance citing a rule is not a finding that the rule was violated. That determination belongs to the Idaho State Bar. Tier: C for every underlying allegation; the citations themselves are A.
Honest-law note — HIPAA. HIPAA has no private right of action. It is enforced by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. The filings use HIPAA as a records-access frame — the executed release, the concealed documentation, the denied power of attorney. The enforceable claims for the same conduct do not run through HIPAA. They run through the ADA, the civil rights statutes, and the constitutional provisions above. Nothing in this document claims a HIPAA violation as a standalone private claim.

7. The Mail — The Most Checkable Claim in the File

The mail allegation is the single most concrete and most checkable claim in the package, and it is the one the movement has already put to its members. The MacGregor grievance alleges that the prosecutor issued unauthorized directives to jail administration requiring all of the detainee’s incoming and outgoing legal and personal mail to be routed through and explicitly approved by the prosecutor’s office prior to delivery — and that through this interception scheme the detainee was denied access to incoming HIPAA medical release forms.

The movement’s Idaho jail-mail poll, live November 11, 2026, asks exactly this question: “Should Idaho County Jail ever route an inmate’s personal mail through the Prosecuting Attorney’s office before it reaches the inmate?” The poll arose from a post stating the jail’s position: personal mail is handled by jail staff and the Sheriff’s Office, never via the prosecutor; legal mail gets special handling.

Those two statements do not contradict each other on their face. The jail’s public position describes general practice. The grievance alleges a specific directive for one inmate. What would settle it is not another statement — it is the paper: the jail’s written mail policy, any written directive concerning the detainee’s mail, and the handling record for his mail specifically. Until that paper is in hand, the allegation is tier C, and the poll is a question to the members, not evidence of the answer. The demand stands regardless: produce the directive, or produce the record showing there never was one. There is no third option.

The institutions: a pattern-or-practice investigation by the DOJ Civil Rights Division under CRIPA; review by Idaho POST and the Idaho Attorney General; the release of the detainee’s complete medical and administrative records; an independent medical evaluation; and judicial oversight in the District of Idaho — up to a special master and closure evaluation, on the FCI Dublin model — if either jail cannot be brought to standard.

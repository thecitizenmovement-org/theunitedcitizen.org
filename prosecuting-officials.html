10. Linda

Where "judicially" came from, and the case in full
Josh

Hana

Linda was an unmarried mother in Texas. She wanted the father of her child prosecuted for not paying child support. The Court said she had no standing to demand his prosecution.

Josh

Tell me more about this Linda.

Hana

Linda R.S. was the mother of a daughter born out of wedlock in Texas. She said Richard D. was the father and he refused to pay child support. Texas Penal Code Article 602 made it a misdemeanor — up to two years in county jail — for any parent to willfully fail to support a child under 18. But Texas courts read it as applying only to parents of legitimate children. Fathers of illegitimate children were excluded entirely. The local district attorney refused to prosecute Richard D., saying Article 602 didn't apply to him. An assistant DA even filed an affidavit saying the state couldn't prosecute under the statute because of how the courts had construed it. Linda sued in federal court — a class action for herself, her daughter, and everyone in the same situation. She argued the interpretation discriminated between legitimate and illegitimate children and violated the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment. She wanted the court to declare Article 602 unconstitutional as applied only to fathers of legitimate children, and to order the DA to prosecute Richard D. A three-judge district court in the Northern District of Texas dismissed her case for lack of standing. The Supreme Court affirmed 5–4. Marshall's reasoning: even if the DA prosecuted Richard D., it would only put him in jail — it wouldn't put child support money in Linda's hands. So prosecuting him wouldn't fix her injury. That's why he said she had no 'judicially cognizable interest' in his prosecution. Justice White dissented, joined by Douglas. His words: 'Under Art. 602, they are rendered nonpersons; a father may ignore them with full knowledge that he will be subjected to no penal sanctions.'

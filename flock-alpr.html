People are saying no to plate cameras — in their own words

This page collects only the opposition: residents speaking against Flock and other automated license-plate readers (ALPRs), the rebuttals they make to pro-camera claims, the votes that removed cameras, and the groups and petitions organizing against them. No vendor claims, no success stories — except where opponents answer them directly.

The local fight is live, not finished. On October 27, 2026, Shasta County supervisors voted 4–1 to require a detailed sheriff's presentation on Flock, with contract termination possible if it falls short — a decision aimed for before the end of November 2026. No Shasta or Tehama vote to remove cameras was found in this research.

Shasta & Tehama — the local record

What residents in the two counties have actually said and done, as reported. Opposition here is organized and on the record — but as of this research, neither county has voted to remove a single camera.

Shasta County · Oct. 27, 2026

Residents give the board an earful

At the October 27, 2026 Board of Supervisors meeting, residents turned out against the county's Flock cameras. The Record Searchlight counted 25 speakers, all but two opposing — some of them school-aged children. Shasta Unfiltered counted 26 speakers, only two in support. The two counts differ; the near-unanimity does not.

The board voted 4–1 (motion by Supervisor Kevin Crye; Supervisor Allen Long voting no) to direct Sheriff Michael Johnson to present on Flock at a future meeting — as early as November 10 — after which the board may vote to terminate the contract. The contract background reported in the article: a five-year, $551,000 Flock contract approved unanimously on November 6, 2025, with about two dozen cameras installed the following January.

“What safeguards prevent someone from using that information to stalk, harass or target them?”
— Stephanie Montgomery, speaking against the cameras, on tracking teen drivers' habits and patterns (via Record Searchlight)

Shasta Unfiltered reported speaker Ellie calling the cameras a “Trojan Horse, desensitizing us to mass surveillance,” speaker Michelle opposing warrantless searches and mass surveillance, and speaker Ashley stating she had a petition with over 1,000 signatures in opposition. The board set a 30-day termination-notice clause in view, with a decision aimed for before the end of November and funding covered through June.

Sources: Record Searchlight, Oct. 27, 2026 · Shasta Unfiltered, Oct. 27, 2026 meeting report

Petition · Anderson & Shasta County

“Flock Off Shasta County”

The Change.org petition “Flock Off Shasta County – Ban Flock Safety cameras in Shasta County, CA” (published August 19, 2026; updated October 8, 2026) demands: halt expansion and renewals, complete removal of Flock cameras from public roads and property in Anderson and throughout Shasta County, and permanent deletion of historically collected location data and vehicle fingerprints. Its case, in its own words: warrantless mass tracking of every vehicle, private corporate cloud storage, and public roads turned into automated checkpoints. It is addressed to the Anderson City Council and Shasta County community leaders.

A companion petition site, “Flock Off SC,” carries the same demands and warns: “Please do not sign this petition if you have signed our petition in person or on change.org” — confirming in-person and online petitions are running in parallel.

Signature counts are not shown on either petition page as fetched. The “over 1,000” figure above is a meeting speaker's claim, not either page's tally.

Sources: Change.org petition · Flock Off SC petition site

The rebuttals — opponents answer the claims

The only place pro-camera claims appear on this page is here, as the claim being answered — each rebuttal quoted or closely paraphrased and attributed to its source.

Claim: “It solves crime / keeps us safe”

The civil-liberties cost outweighs the benefit

“For us, the threat to our civil liberties was greater than any benefit we could get from the flawed product.”
— Santa Cruz Mayor Fred Keeley, at the Jan. 13, 2026 termination (via Permit Atlas summary of the council record)
“You can't surveil your way to public safety… but you can not do it in a way that maintains people's civil liberties and freedoms.”
— Gabriel Barraza, Get the Flock Out, Watsonville (City on a Hill Press)

Fight for the Future's “FLOCK Out” petition argues the money should go instead to “resources that are proven to keep communities safe: jobs, housing, health services, education.” San Diego speaker Carmen Valdez (District 9, Youth Will intern) called continuing the contracts “scary, dangerous and creepy”: “It's really sad to see how we're using money to stalk and target vulnerable communities instead of investing it towards solutions” such as mental health care, transit, accessibility, and affordable housing. Sources: Fight for the Future · Daylight San Diego

Denver, handled carefully: opponents cite Denver Open Data / 9NEWS figures via Consumer Rights Wiki — motor-vehicle theft of ~430 in April 2025 (cameras on) vs. ~301–313 in April 2026 (cameras off), a ~29–30% decrease, while clearance by arrest fell 13% to 3%. The same source explicitly warns the year-over-year change “cannot be attributed to the camera lapse alone” and rests on a single month. It is not proof cameras increase crime, and this page does not present it that way. Consumer Rights Wiki

Claim: “Data is not shared with ICE / out-of-state / federal agencies”

The audit logs say otherwise

Marin County: the EFF reported audit logs showing the Marin Sheriff shared ALPR data 254,131 times in November 2024 alone, including agencies in Alabama, Indiana, Kentucky, Florida, and Texas — sharing the EFF says violates California SB 34, and, for immigration enforcement, SB 54. “ALPRs are cameras that capture images of vehicles and upload their location to a searchable, shareable database. They are a mass surveillance technology that collects data indiscriminately on every vehicle on the road.” EFF, Oct. 2026

Richmond: out-of-state and federal agencies queried Richmond Flock data around 1.5 million times, including for immigration-related purposes, per an EFF presentation to the council — about 500 FBI searches in one period, at least 275 referencing immigration authorities. CC Pulse, Oct. 8, 2026

The ICE hotlist: the EFF notes Flock systems commonly compare plates against FBI NCIC hotlists, and the “Immigration Violator” hotlist is populated exclusively by ICE, including plates tied to administrative warrants issued by ICE agents without judicial review. EFF, June 2026

Monterey & Mountain View: Monterey County's vote followed reporting that county Flock data was accessible to out-of-state agencies in 2025 despite state law (the sheriff blamed a Flock configuration error). Mountain View's cameras went off after a police audit found federal and state access in violation of city policy. Sources: California City News · City of Mountain View

“Even when used exactly as intended, Flock cameras are an affront to our privacy… Local flock data has been directly shared with Trump's FBI as recently as May of 2026… the only way to protect this highly sensitive data is to not produce it in the first place.”
— Humboldt Against A.I. Surveillance Hell (HAASH), via Lost Coast Outpost

DeFlock Monongalia's answer to “no federal agencies have access”: Flock makes the claim but “they don't put it in the contract”; audit logs show federally funded HIDTA task forces, the WV Fusion Center, and regional networks searching county data — and “no audit log can see an agent using a partner agency's account.” DeFlock Monongalia

Claim: “Safeguards, audits, and retention limits protect you”

Moving data is not deleting it; audits document abuse after the fact

The EFF's analysis of San Francisco's ALPR policy: the 30-day move of data from vendor to city servers is “moving data is not deleting it”; the policy does not require a warrant or judicial authorization; an incident/CAD number is “not judicial authorization”; and audit-log improvements “can expose abuse only after a search has occurred.” For contrast, New Hampshire requires deletion within 3 minutes; Flock's default was 7 days. Via State of Surveillance briefing, Oct. 2, 2026

DeFlock Illinois' talking points: “Retention limits are just another overridable default” — one sheriff set 30 days, then said within days he was “reevaluating” it, while the vendor moved its own default to 7. “Audits don't stop misuse — they only document it after the fact. A Mooresville officer ran 31 searches to stalk a private citizen; the audit logged every one and prevented none.” The reason field is self-entered. “You cannot regulate a warrantless dragnet into something that honors a warrant.” DeFlock IL talking points

The EFF's analysis also found search reasons including “LMAO”; the ACLU says Flock's Audit Assistance effectiveness “had not been independently verified” and should be assumed ineffective — “window dressing” — via Consumer Rights Wiki summaries. Consumer Rights Wiki

Claim: “It is not warrantless tracking / not a search”

A federal judge called it indiscriminate mass surveillance

In United States v. Kyle (N.D. Okla., Oct. 1, 2026), U.S. District Judge Sara E. Hill called Flock a “type of indiscriminate mass surveillance”: “Freedom from persistent, dragnet-style surveillance while in public is not a foreign concept in our society, and it is a reasonable expectation that society already accepts.” She ruled that a warrantless search of more than 50 records over about a month, across multiple states — a deputy queried after seeing a California plate, before observing a violation — violated the Fourth Amendment, and suppressed the evidence. Limits, stated plainly: it is a trial-court ruling, not binding precedent; most other Oklahoma federal courts had reached the opposite conclusion; and the decision did not outlaw the technology outright. Sources: Daily Caller · Military.com

“The issue isn't one snapshot; it's that enough snapshots become a map of your life… They record where we were, when we were there, and over time can reveal our religious practices, medical visits, political activities, personal relationships and daily routines.”
— ACLU San Diego, PANA v. San Diego, filed Sept. 29, 2026 — demanding the city cease ALPR use, uninstall cameras, and delete data, under the California Constitution's privacy right (ACLU San Diego)

The Shasta petition's own language: Flock “continuously log[s], photograph[s], and track[s] the movement of every single vehicle that passes them… without a warrant or individualized suspicion,” “turning public roads into automated checkpoints.” Change.org And Monterey speaker Miles Wade: “Law enforcement sees judicial process as a hurdle that is inconvenient, and they don't want to deal with that. And so they want to bypass that and be able to surveil people without a warrant.” Via LA Post / KSBW

Claim: “Misidentification is rare / alerts are verified”

Wrong plates, gunpoint stops, a month in jail

Hugo Parra, San Diego — opponents' central example: police had no plate, only a vehicle description and witness ID. A Flock “hit” allegedly showed Parra five miles away from the Golden Hill attempted carjacking at the time of the crime, before any pursuit began. Parra spent nearly a month in jail before assault/evasion charges were dropped. He and driver Ariel Beltran are suing, seeking $1.5M each — a claim, not a judgment. Sources: Slashdot / YRO · Daylight San Diego

LAPD audit: the Police Commission suspended LAPD Flock use in July 2026 after an Inspector General audit found officers improperly investigated 161 people whose vehicles were wrongly flagged as stolen. Via Face Not Found

Other misread examples opponents list (each attributed, not verified in this research): a York County, SC motorist detained at gunpoint after a one-character misread (stolen flag); Morristown, TN grandparents detained at gunpoint after “O” was read as “0,” with a 3-year-old watching; a Toledo, OH driver detained at gunpoint, a dog used, jailed for hours after “7” was read as “2.” Via Wikipedia — Flock Safety

Speaker claim, attributed as such: at San Diego, Elizabeth Gibbon cited a Vallejo police study finding up to 37% of fixed-position ALPR reads were misreads. Daylight San Diego

Abuse tallies differ — do not merge them: 241 documented ALPR abuse incidents in one DeFlock summary; at least 27 cases since 2018 of innocent motorists stopped, detained at gunpoint, or jailed over ALPR error in another; 28 cases since 2024 of officers using Flock to stalk in another. Sources: DeFlock Monongalia · Max Intel — documented ALPR misuse cases

Claim: “It is cheap / worth the cost”

Opponents read the price tags back

Opponents point to the contract prices themselves as the cost of a contested system: Shasta $551,000 over 5 years; Monterey's rejected $1.7M over 10 years; Fullerton ~$51,000/year; El Cerrito $105,000/year; Oakland's proposed $2.25M over 2 years; San Diego ~$2M. Their argument (above) is that the money should go to jobs, housing, health, education, mental health, and transit instead. No opponent source reviewed provides a cost-effectiveness study comparing ALPR to those alternatives — it is presented here as an argument, not a finding.

Traffic cameras — a separate fight

Red-light and speed cameras are automated ticketing cameras — a different technology from Flock/ALPR plate-tracking networks. They are kept in this section only, not mixed into the sections above. Milford, CT paused both together and is flagged as such.
